Thalassophobia

In gameplay testing

A crew of up to four divers takes salvage jobs from outside clients. Nobody tells you what is down there. You find out by being near it.

One client wants the flight recorder from a crashed tiltrotor, another wants evidence from a missing submarine. Every contract is handcrafted but plays out differently from run to run. The crew sails out, dives, works by torchlight and comes back to get paid, if they come back. A quiet, successful job is a valid ending too.

The boat is the crew's home. You pick a job from the board in the cabin, load the gear and sail to the site. Down below, everyone breathes from their own tanks plus one shared rebreather pack, so where the crew sets that pack down shapes the whole dive. Bigger jobs split the crew: on The Long Drop one player stays topside and runs the winch while the others ride the cage into the dark.

The animals are not scripted to attack. They live in the level, and they react to light, noise and what they can sense of you. The same wreck can be an uneventful salvage one night and a very bad one the next.

I direct the game, design its contracts and write its systems. The game started in Unreal; when I adopted AI-assisted coding we moved it to Unity, where we iterate much faster.

Decision · design, sound and code

Fear has to be fair

My rule for every encounter: nothing is telegraphed, and nothing is unfair. A threat shows itself through a sound, a shape or fish scattering before it bites, never through a banner or a boss bar.

Our sound artist made most of the game's sound and music, from pressure beds and creature calls to the score. I built the system that decides when it plays. The music follows what each diver has actually seen, heard or touched, thickening as their sanity drops. It is not allowed to announce a creature nobody has perceived.

  1. ExploringCalm bed. Depth sets the colour: the deep bed fades in between 25 and 80 m.
  2. DangerOnly when this diver sees, hears or touches a threat. Pads thicken as sanity drops.
  3. ContactA stinger on the first real contact, then the fight layers.
  4. RecoveryTwenty seconds of quieter music after a fight, then back to exploring.

A creature nobody has noticed can still drain a diver's sanity, but it never gets to raise the music.

Decision · tools and marketing

Footage from the real game

Our marketing needs a steady flow of footage, and nobody should have to fake it. I built a capture tool so our marketing collaborator can describe a shot and get it from the game itself. An AI agent turns the request into a short shot script, places divers, creatures and camera in a real level, renders clean clips and stills, and checks every frame for problems such as a camera inside a rock. An edit step cuts vertical Shorts with captions and the game's own sounds.

The rule I set for it: no generative AI in anything a viewer sees or hears.

Decision · production

People play it before we build more

Within a week of starting in Unity, the whole loop of boat, dive, contract, return and payout ran end to end, solo and in four-player co-op, under our automated checks. The first real playtest still found what the checks missed: an opaque first minute on one mission and co-op performance problems on another. So I froze new content until two reference missions pass a human playtest.

Shelf-Employed

One spell went wrong in a wizard's warehouse. Now 4,832 boxes are on the floor, and you put every one back where it belongs.

Two of us made it: my co-developer, an artist, made the visual design, and I did the programming, gameplay systems and audio implementation, with music and sound effects licensed from asset vendors. We released it under the DuoQ label.

It was our first test of releasing a smaller stylized game: store page, launch, achievements and support, at a size two people could carry. When an intro title card read as AI-made, I cut it. The look had to be ours.

Decision · after launch

Ten languages, picked from data

We launched in English. After launch I picked a first wave of ten translations from Steam's own hardware-survey language data rather than gut feeling, and built a kit that exports every string and checks each language on the way back in. The game now lists twelve languages on Steam.

Two games in alpha · WIP names

Two small co-op games, built side by side

They look and play nothing alike. Underneath, they share the expensive part: a character pipeline, so each game can have a cast that reacts without a character team behind it.

SaunaWeekend WIP name

Alpha

Two to four animal friends on a sauna raft in the middle of a frozen lake, playing sauna versions of poker, roulette and push-your-luck cards. Every round ends with water on the stones, and the heat hits everyone the same. Win snacks, drinks and a vihta to stand the heat, or push the others out. The last one left in the sauna wins.

MoneyBin WIP name

Alpha

A first-person co-op heist for up to eight robbers. The crew's drilling machine jams inside a vault holding billions of real coins, and its hose only swallows what you bring it. Shove, shovel, barrow and mow money to it, then automate with belts, pipes and robots. The deeper the money, the harder the haul.

Shared production decision

One character pipeline for both games

Characters are the most expensive thing in a small stylized game, and both of these need a cast whose faces react to the game. So I built a character pipeline with AI-assisted coding. Its current form is the Cast Workshop: a tool inside the game that takes the artist's base model through eyes, mouth, face rig, expressions and body poses, then adds the character to the game, without anyone touching code.

Six characters have gone through it for the sauna game in three days. They are playable at the table now; hand-painting and final sign-off are still to come. The tool passes its end-to-end acceptance run and is still being developed.

The six in the Workshop, all on the same "laugh" expression. Hand-painting is still to come.

The split matters to me. People decide what each character looks like, do the hand-painting and give the final yes or no; no AI-generated texture ships. AI-assisted coding built the Workshop's steps and the acceptance run that creates a throwaway character, adds it to the game and removes it again. The result is a reacting cast for two small games without a character team, and one path every new character follows.

In testing · Survival

Nordheim WIP name

The Sampo is broken and its pieces have washed ashore. You wake beside an old offering stone in the Finnish lake country and go looking for them.

Nordheim is a survival game in the Valheim family: one large generated world, lands of rising danger, and a guardian in each whose defeat opens the next. You fell pines, build a shelter, hunt, craft better tools and push further out.

Everything inside that structure comes from Finnish nature and the Kalevala. The first guardian is Otso, the honey-paw, called to his skull pine with an offering of honey. Night brings menninkäiset out of the spruce, and a näkki waits in the lake shallows. You leave a share of your hunt on the offering stone for Tapio's favour, and you rest better after löyly in a sauna you built yourself.

I make it with a programmer and an artist. The world is the part we are testing first, because the forest has to feel like home to a Finn before anything else matters. The traveller and the creatures are still placeholders. Testing decides whether it earns a funding push.

Decision · direction

Valheim's structure, the Kalevala's content

I split the game in two on purpose. The structure is borrowed and proven. Every name, creature, ritual and goal has to come from Finnish folklore, written in Finnish with an English gloss, so a Finnish player recognises it and everyone else learns it.

I also drew one line early. The north of the map is Pohjola, the mythic land of the Kalevala. It is not Sámi religion, and Sámi sacred practice is not used as game mechanics or loot.

How I work

From writing the code to leading the games

I studied game programming and software development (B.Eng., Kajaani University of Applied Sciences) and founded Varattu Valo Games while I was still studying. Since then I've gone from writing most of the code myself to leading several games at once with people in art, sound, design and marketing.

Running a company next to my studies taught me to plan under pressure. Now I set the direction for each game, sequence the work across all of them, and build the tools that take the slow, repetitive parts away.

  • DirectionI give each game one rule it lives by, like Thalassophobia's "nothing telegraphed, nothing unfair", and judge the work against it.
  • PlanningStore pages, playtests and launches are separate milestones, and no date goes public before testing supports it.
  • ToolsCapture, test and character tools, built quickly with AI coding agents. Creative calls and final approval stay with people.
